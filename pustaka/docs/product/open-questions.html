Product

Open questions

Twenty-three decisions the BRD refuses to invent, each with the options actually available, the trade-off each one carries, and what it blocks. Eighteen are answered — the entire commercial block, the licence, the budget guard, the submodule boundary, and now what success means, what Mission Control is, the provider strategy, tenancy, the database version, and which CLI ships first. Three of the five still open exist because those answers were given.

How to answer

Answer by OQ id, one at a time, in any order. An answer does not have to be one of the listed options; the options exist so that "none of these, here is what I actually want" is an easy thing to say. Each answer is written into the decision log as the next D- number, dated the day it was decided, and this page's row flips to Answered → D-nnn. Answers are expected to open new questions — that is not a failure of the answer, it is what moving from a shape to its parameters looks like.

What the 2026-08-08 block changed

GAOS is open core, self-hosted: the repository is given away and works on its own, private submodules may add capability but are never required, and revenue comes from installation, support and licensed add-ons rather than from hosting (D-026…D-034). Three questions that were blocked by that unknown are now merely open, and are marked narrowed below: OQ-11 authentication, OQ-16 high availability, and OQ-23 the supported CLIs — the last of which has since been answered outright (D-049). Nothing about their difficulty changed; what changed is whose problem each one is.

Later the same day the owner closed two more. OQ-20: the repository is Apache-2.0 with a DCO sign-off and a reserved name, so it can be published (D-035). OQ-18: the budget cap becomes a three-zone guard that paces spend across the period and defers work rather than failing it (D-037). And CLI-versus-API stopped being either/or — both are supported modes behind one master switch (D-036).

Commercial

Eleven questions. The first eight were answered together on 2026-08-08 and are kept here in full, because a register that deletes what it resolves teaches nothing. The last three are new, each one a direct consequence of an answer above it — and two of those are now answered too, leaving the rate card as the only commercial question still open.

OQ-01 · What is GAOS commercially?

Why it was open: the schema declares tenants, plans and budget caps, and the prototype ships two tenant fixtures. Nothing stated whether those tenants were strangers who pay, colleagues in one organisation, or an abstraction that would never be exercised.

Answered as D-026: option C, in the form that has no hosted tier. The repository is open source and anyone who clones it can self-host and use it as it is — and it works as it is, which is now a rule rather than a description (BR-27). Some core capability will live in separate private submodules sold commercially; none of it may ever be required for this repository to function. Setting GAOS up for somebody is itself a commercial service. Hosted SaaS was rejected on infrastructure grounds and internal-only on evidence grounds — the core bet needs users the owner did not recruit.

What it changed: the tenant boundary now separates colleagues inside one operator's deployment rather than paying strangers, which downgraded R-02 from blocking to serious and narrowed OQ-11. It also opened OQ-19 and OQ-20, neither of which existed while the commercial model did not.

OQ-02 · Who is the target customer?

Why it was open: two very different buyers were consistent with the same design. A governance buyer wants the audit trail and the readiness rubric — the refusal to plan against a thin brief is the feature. A throughput buyer wants more shipped per week and experiences the same refusal as friction.

Answered as D-027: researchers, small companies, and individual experts — in IT and outside it. None of them is the enterprise governance buyer the design would also have suited, and that is the useful part of the answer. It means no procurement process, no security questionnaire, no certification gate, and no sales motion: the acquisition surface is documentation and an install that works.

It also resolves the governance-versus-throughput tension rather than picking a side. For a researcher the audit trail is reproducibility — the same capability, wanted for a reason that has nothing to do with compliance. And the non-IT expert is the persona that makes the service business coherent: someone who can write the brief the readiness rubric wants, and cannot stand up a stack to feed it to.

OQ-03 · Is an external auditor a first-class user?

Why it was open: GAOS can already answer "why does this code exist?" by walking from a commit back to a sentence in the back office. Whether anyone outside the tenant was meant to ask that question had never been decided, and an auditor persona implies exported evidence bundles, retention beyond the 90-day window, read-only credentials, and a tamper-evidence story for the event stream.

Answered as D-028, derived from the two answers above rather than decided on its own: no. A self-hosted deployment owned by a researcher or a small team has no third-party auditor relationship to serve, and building the persona would add four requirement clusters to a product with no users. The replay trail stays as an internal explainability and reproducibility feature, which is what the actual buyer wants from it.

The deferred work is not discarded — it is the most obvious first private submodule, and it appears as option (A) in OQ-19. That is the pattern to expect from this register: a capability the core does not need becomes a candidate for the paid boundary rather than a deletion.

OQ-04 · What are the plans, and what does each limit?

Why it was open: Tenant.plan is a declared string, and there was no signup, no plan selection, no usage view and no billing to give it meaning.

Answered in two parts, because the owner's answer contained two decisions.

Part one — D-032: the quota properties are instruments for monitoring model usage, not billing tiers. Three named profiles ship as seed data — solo, standard (the DDL's existing defaults of 8 concurrent tasks and a 500 USD cap, which were there all along and this document previously described as absent) and unmetered, which warns rather than caps and must be chosen deliberately. Profiles are operator-edited; requirement J-7's self-service surface is withdrawn rather than deferred, and a new J-8 requires usage to be visible without writing a query.

Part two — D-033: in the MVP, models are invoked through their command-line tools, not their APIs. That is why the cost figures need an estimation path at all: a CLI may report no usage, so every figure now carries a basis, measured or estimated, and the two are never silently summed. It is also why every agent must be instructed to produce a structured handoff document — with no response body to read, that document is the only way one agent's output becomes another's input. The full mechanism, the required document shape and the flowchart the owner asked for are BRD §7.6.

Extended the same day by D-036: the API path is a supported mode rather than a later replacement. Both adapters sit behind one master configuration, the handoff document is required in both, and development still starts with the CLI. Nothing in D-033 is reversed — what changes is that "CLI, not API" became "CLI first, API supported, one switch".

What it opened: OQ-22 (where the numbers come from when the tool reports nothing) and OQ-23 (which tools are supported and what each must guarantee). It also produced R-12, which is the honest cost of this route and is stated there in full.

OQ-05 · What is the pricing basis?

Why it was open: model spend is a cost of goods that varies per task by an order of magnitude, and a pricing basis not coupled to that variance loses money on exactly the customers who use the product most.

Answered as D-029: the variance problem disappears, because OQ-06 moves the spend to the operator. What is sold is installation, support, submodule licences and custom work — no seats, no tasks, no tokens, no margin on anything. A published, fixed-scope rate card is proposed in BRD §8.11.1: three installation packages, three support tiers of which the first is free, a per-install annual submodule licence with a stated academic and individual reduction, and a day rate for everything else.

The reasoning behind the shapes is with the rate card rather than here, but the short version is that this buyer does not enter a quote process for a small install, cannot evaluate an hourly estimate, and will use something else rather than email for an academic discount. The numbers themselves are proposed, not committed — OQ-21.

OQ-06 · Who pays the model provider?

Why it was open: it changes what a budget cap means, and whether GAOS ever holds a provider credential belonging to a customer.

Answered as D-030: the repository is open source and self-hosted, so the operator always pays, on their own account. GAOS never resells tokens, never holds anyone else's credential, and is never in the billing path (BR-29). Anyone may fork, download, reuse and contribute.

The consequence worth stating twice: budgetCapUsd now protects the operator's money. Shipping an unenforced cap stopped being a financial exposure for the project and became a duty-of-care failure toward users — a worse category, because the people exposed did not make the decision. R-05 is reframed accordingly and OQ-18 is narrowed by it.

OQ-07 · What service level and support model?

Why it was open: the honest position was one public edge machine, one private VM, and one ArcadeDB node with no replica. Any uptime commitment made before that changed would have been unmeetable.

Answered as D-031, and the earlier framing turned out to be the wrong question: after OQ-01, GAOS hosts nothing, so an availability SLA would be a promise about somebody else's machine. Support therefore commits to response times, not uptime, within stated business hours in one named timezone. Community support is free and public and stays that way; Standard and Priority add a private channel, a first-response target, an annual allowance of remote assistance and upgrade help.

Two constraints on the model, both structural rather than generous. No paid tier ever gates a bug fix — fixes land in the open repository for everyone, and what a subscription buys is attention, not correctness. And no safety control, guard, cap or audit record is ever sold at all (BR-28).

OQ-14 · Data residency and tenant data handling

Why it was open: a tenant's repository contents, prompts and diffs pass through GAOS and onward to a model provider, and nothing stated where data lived, for how long, who could read it, whether it might train anything, or what happened on deletion.

Answered as D-034, and written out as a full requirement domain — BRD §8.12, domain L, eleven requirements with a data inventory. The short form: residency is whatever the operator's machines are, because the project holds nothing after OQ-01. That is the easy half.

The hard half is egress, and it is worth stating plainly because a data-handling page that does not say it is misleading: what leaves the host is source code, selected automatically by a traversal, including files nobody reviewed before they were sent — and the selection widens on every retry, because widening the slice is what a retry does. So the commitment is not a location. It is a published egress inventory, a per-provider data-handling profile the operator fills in and the UI displays, a pre-dispatch redaction and allowlist pass that refuses a dispatch rather than scoring the leak afterwards, a supported no-egress mode for work under confidentiality, and a deletion story that survives an append-only event stream by destroying the payload behind an EXTERNAL reference while the event keeps the reference. Support engagements get no standing access to any customer system.

OQ-19 · What goes into a private submodule, and what never can?

Why it was open: D-026 said private submodules exist and said nothing about what was in them. This was the question that decided whether the open core stays trustworthy: the failure mode that ends open-core projects is a boundary that moves, and it moves one reasonable-looking release at a time (R-10).

Six candidates, with the recommendation and the reasoning, were laid out in BRD §16. In brief: the compliance and evidence pack was the strongest first choice, because OQ-03 had just removed exactly that cluster from the core, a small company asks for it and a researcher never does, and none of it is a safety control. An identity pack was the natural second, with the caveat that the core's local accounts must stay genuinely sufficient rather than deliberately weak. A provider pack that puts cost visibility behind a paywall was the option to reject on principle, since the operator is the one paying. And none of the above was a legitimate answer: services-only is the simplest business and the honest default until a customer asks twice for the same missing thing.

What the answer had to contain, whichever it was: the list, the price, and — for each item — the sentence describing what the core does without it. Two rules already constrained it, BR-27 and BR-28.

Answered as D-038: a generation pack, under the rule manual-free, generation-paid — a seventh candidate rather than any of the six listed. The core keeps accepting, storing, rendering and scoring manually-authored versions of every artifact in the product master record — the narrative brief, behaviour diagrams and data models, user stories, acceptance criteria, test cases and test scenarios — and the submodule sells the work of producing them: diagrams generated from the narrative record, diagrams derived from other diagrams, and AI-generated stories, criteria, test cases and scenarios. Every item passes the sentence this page demanded: without the submodule, you author it yourself, and the product scores it the same either way. That construction is why it beat the compliance pack for first place — the paid half is automation of something the free half must keep doing manually, so the boundary R-10 worries about cannot drift. The compliance and identity packs stay on the candidate list for later. The price is the rate card's existing submodule line, still proposed pending OQ-21.

OQ-20 · Licence, contribution policy and trademark

Why it was open: there was no LICENSE file in the repository. Until there was, "open source" was an intention, contributions could not be accepted, and nobody could safely fork — which made this the one part of D-026 that blocked publishing at all, not merely blocking something later.

Answered as D-035, and shipped in the same change: Apache-2.0 in LICENSE, a DCO sign-off in CONTRIBUTING.md, and a trademark reservation in NOTICE. Apache-2.0 because the buyer in OQ-02 has to be able to adopt it without asking a lawyer, and because its patent grant is worth more to a small company than a copyleft clause many of them refuse on policy. DCO rather than CLA because a CLA exists to enable relicensing and the paid components live in separate repositories the project already owns outright — so there is nothing to relicense, and asking contributors to sign paperwork for an unused power is a cost with no purpose. The name is the one thing kept back: use the code, rename the fork.

What it costs, stated once because it cannot be undone: every future contribution arrives under Apache-2.0, and changing the licence later would need every contributor's consent. And a permissive licence lets anyone — including someone with more capacity than the owner — host GAOS as a service. Since hosting was refused as a business in OQ-01, that costs little in practice, but it is a real door left open on purpose rather than an oversight.

The options that were rejected are kept below, because a licence choice is exactly the kind of decision whose alternatives get re-litigated later by someone who was not in the room.

OptionGood forCost

Commercial value stays where D-026 put it — in the submodules and the services — rather than in licence restrictionsAnyone, including someone with more capacity than the owner, may host GAOS as a service. The vendored web fonts and charting library in pustaka/docs/assets/ were named in NOTICE without their full texts stored beside them; since 2026-08-20 each text sits in the directory it covers, fetched from upstream rather than reconstructed, along with ECharts' NOTICE — which section 4(d) requires separately — and the BSD 3-Clause text for the d3 code ECharts embeds and its own licence points at. deploy/check-stacks.sh asserts the pairing before every deploy, so the gap cannot reopen by omission. See T-140.

OQ-21 · Confirm the rate card, and decide how it is invoiced

Why it is open: the numbers in BRD §8.11.1 were proposed by an agent at the owner's request, not chosen by the owner. They are the only invented figures anywhere in the BRD, which is why they are labelled on the page itself.

Confirming them means answering four things the numbers cannot answer on their own: which currency is quoted and who absorbs conversion; whether the installation packages survive contact with a customer whose environment is not a clean host; what the invoicing mechanism is for a solo maintainer selling internationally — payment processor, taxes, and which jurisdiction's terms of service the customer accepts; and what happens when a fixed-scope install runs over, which is the single most common way this kind of offer loses money.

A cheaper alternative to answering it in the abstract: publish the rate card, sell one Starter install, and let the first engagement correct the numbers. That is also the fastest route to O-7.

Product & scope

OQ-08 · What measurable success targets apply?

Why it was open: a standard BRD requires numeric criteria. GAOS had no baseline — no users, no tenants, no run against a real model, and the one defined measurement had never been performed. The realistic sequencing was: run the second-run measurement once, then set targets against what it produces. Confirming that sequencing was one available answer to this question.

D-026 made it harder, not easier: runs now happen on other people's machines, so the measurement depends on operators choosing to report it. Whether to ask them, and how, was part of this question too.

Answered as D-039: success is conformance, not a business number. The target is that the system as developed builds and runs without issue and passes every acceptance criterion and every scenario in the QA test plan — positive and negative alike, every case in the register — plus the three §4.1 acceptance tests. It is a pass/fail target on purpose. Numeric outcome targets stay unset, because the sequencing above is still true and a percentage invented against no baseline measures whoever chose it rather than the product. What the answer adds to that sequencing is a decision rather than a wait: the register already enumerates what "working" means, case by case, so there is a real target today and it is not the absence of one. Numbers become settable after the second-run measurement has been performed once against a real model, which after D-026 arrives when an operator volunteers it. D-025 names who runs the checks in the meantime.

OQ-12 · Band-driven autonomy, or gates on by default?

Why it is open: the design makes autonomy a function of record completeness — a coherent position, and the whole reason the readiness rubric has teeth. But a new team with a thin brief meets a wall on day one. Turning gates on by default until an operator opts out is gentler and weakens the incentive that makes the rubric work. This is a product-experience decision, not a safety one; both options keep the money, auth and schema gates.

D-027 sharpens it: a lone researcher installing GAOS on a laptop meets that wall with nobody to ask, and no support contract. The wall is still defensible — it is the product's thesis — but it now needs to explain itself unusually well.

OQ-15 · What is Mission Control for?

Why it was open: it is the most finished thing in the repository — ported, tested, deployed — and the build order says it should have been ninth. If it was the surface operators use, it needed authentication, per-tenant scoping and accessibility work before anything else was added to it. If it was an internal console and the API the product, it could stay as it was while the loops beneath it got built, which is what the build order implies.

Since D-026 nobody buys a surface at all, which removed the commercial argument from both sides and left the build-order argument standing alone. Domain L added one concrete requirement to whichever answer won: the provider data-handling profile and the egress inventory have to be visible somewhere, and Mission Control is the only somewhere that exists.

Answered as D-040: both — one surface, two audiences, separated by role rather than by codebase. There is no second console and no fork; an internal capability an operator does not get is a permission, never a build. The answer costs what the question said it would cost, and the cost is accepted rather than deferred: authentication (OQ-11), per-tenant scoping and accessibility become preconditions on further Mission Control work. The build order is not overridden — the loops beneath still get built first — but the next feature added to this surface comes after those three rather than before them. Domain L's requirement lands here by elimination, which is what L-3 already assumed. Recorded as I-8.

Technical policy

OQ-09 · One model provider, or several?

Why it was open: the model gateway exists as a layer whose stated prohibition is leaking provider-specific shapes upward — the design anticipated multiple providers. Only one was named anywhere, and no second adapter existed. Multi-provider costs a normalised tier abstraction and per-provider cost accounting, and makes the escalation ladder harder to define, because "one tier above" needs a meaning that spans vendors.

D-033 raised the stakes — execution became coupled to a provider's tool as well as its models, concentrating two dependencies in one vendor instead of one. D-036 took one of them back: with the API a supported mode, a provider whose CLI cannot be driven is still reachable. OQ-23 is the same question asked at the level of the adapter contract, and it is still open.

Answered as D-041: multi-provider behind the gateway, with CLI mode the MVP's focus. Anthropic is the first adapter rather than the only one, and the gateway's prohibition stops being a design intention and gets something to constrain. The two halves are separate and both binding — the architecture is plural from the start, and the MVP effort goes into the CLI adapter, so the first providers to ship are those whose command-line tool clears OQ-23's five-point contract. D-036 is what keeps that focus from being a narrowing: a provider whose CLI cannot be driven is reachable through API mode, so multi-provider is not quietly conditional on every vendor shipping a scriptable tool. The costs named above are accepted rather than argued away, and the escalation ladder is the part that would otherwise break: BR-15's "one model tier above" is meaningless across vendors unless tiers are defined in the gateway's own vocabulary, which is now BR-36.

OQ-10 · Tenancy strategy at scale

Why it was open: the data model presents all three strategies with their costs and recommends database-per-tenant as the default, but explicitly says to pick one and be strict. Picking late is the expensive outcome — the repository layer is written differently for each.

OptionGood forCost
Database per tenant (the document's default)Hard isolation, per-tenant backup and restore, noisy-neighbour containmentA connection pool per tenant; migrations run N times; cross-tenant analytics needs a separate pipeline
Shared database, tenantId everywhereMany small tenants, cross-tenant reporting, one migrationEvery query must filter, forever. One missing WHERE is a data breach rather than a bug
HybridLarge tenants isolated, small ones pooledTwo code paths in the repository layer; only worth it at scale, and there is no scale yet

D-026 changed the weighting without deciding it. A typical deployment now has one operator and a handful of tenants that are project compartments, not strangers — which made the per-database cost look heavy and the shared-database risk look tolerable. The counter-argument survived: the operator who most needs GAOS to be defensible is a small company with client work in separate compartments, and for them the missing WHERE is still a breach.

Answered as D-042: all three stay reachable behind one repository interface, and which one a deployment uses becomes configuration rather than an architectural commitment. Each row of the table above is an implementation of the same interface, and nothing above the repository layer may know which is in use — that prohibition is the whole decision, because without it the abstraction is a naming convention. This narrows D-011 rather than reversing it: database-per-tenant remains the recommended default and the shape the DDL is written for, and tenantId stays declared and indexed on every vertex under every strategy, because that is what makes a misrouted restore detectable wherever the boundary sits. BR-26 is amended to say configurable boundary rather than database boundary first. The cost this question named is not avoided, it is paid: the answer does not pick, it buys the interface up front in exchange for the option to pick later. That trade is only honest while the interface is real, so a strategy that has never been run is a claim rather than a capability.

OQ-11 · Authentication approach

Why it is open: there is none. The back-office login sets a cookie and checks nothing; any value opens it. The question is not whether — it is what shape: local accounts, an identity provider, per-tenant SSO, API tokens for machine access, or some combination.

Narrowed by D-026. It no longer blocks every commercial requirement, because the boundary separates colleagues rather than paying strangers. What it does block is unchanged and is easy to state: nothing may bind beyond localhost until this is answered. The likely answer is now local accounts with real password hashing and session handling in the core, sufficient for a lab or a small team, with an identity-provider integration as candidate (B) in OQ-19 — subject to the constraint recorded there, that the core's own accounts must be sufficient rather than deliberately weak.

Narrowed by D-040. Mission Control is now a surface external operators are expected to sit in front of, which promotes this from an open question to a blocking dependency: authentication, per-tenant scoping and accessibility are preconditions on further Mission Control work rather than items with no date. The shape is still unanswered and the localhost rule above is unchanged. What changed is the consequence of leaving it unanswered — it now stops a named piece of work rather than a hypothetical one.

OQ-17 · Which ArcadeDB version, validated when?

Why it was open: no DDL had ever been applied. The data model warns in its own words that constraint syntax and index options move between releases, and that EXTERNAL properties require v26.5.1 or later. Pinning a target version and running the schema against a scratch database is cheap now and expensive after the first migration exists — it is build-order step 1's real precondition.

Domain L added a requirement to it: L-7's deletion story depends on an EXTERNAL payload being destroyable independently of the record that references it. That behaviour needs verifying on the target version at the same time as everything else, because a deletion promise resting on an unverified storage behaviour is not a promise.

Answered as D-043: the target is ArcadeDB 26.7.2 — the version already running on the infrastructure the dashboard points at, so the target is an observation rather than a forecast, and it clears the v26.5.1 floor by more than a year of releases. The when is the half that was actually in question and it is answered too: the DDL is applied to a scratch database on 26.7.2 before build-order step 1 writes anything, which is the data model's own advice taken at the only moment it is still cheap. Two things are verified in that same pass rather than a later one — that the constraint and index syntax runs as written, and that an EXTERNAL payload can be destroyed independently of the record referencing it, which is what L-7 rests on. The version is pinned rather than floating: an upgrade is a schema event that re-runs the validation.

Two of the four rows above are now answered: the count is local and priced from a configured table, and an unpriceable model produces no cost rather than a guessed one — never a zero, because zero is the answer the budget guard will happily let through. What is left is the row this question was named after and the one it cannot settle alone: where the price table lives, who updates it, and whether a maintained one is core or licensed. That is entangled with BRD §16 option (D) and constrained by BR-28, so it stays here until the submodule question is extended or the owner rules on it. This question is not answered; it is smaller.

Narrowed further by D-051, which undercut its premise. The question assumes the CLI reports nothing. Measured on 2026-08-21, Claude Code reports both: total_cost_usd and a per-model modelUsage breakdown with input, output and cache tokens. So for the first supported adapter there is no estimate to produce — costBasis is measured, the tokenizer row above is moot, and the budget guard is enforced against the tool's own figure. One caveat belongs with that, because it is easy to overclaim: measured under a subscription, total_cost_usd is the API-equivalent price of the work rather than marginal spend, which is why the guard stays a pacing instrument in CLI mode. The question survives anyway, and is now better posed: a price table is still needed for API mode and for any future adapter that reports nothing, so the row it was named after — who maintains that table, and whether a maintained one is core or licensed — is untouched. What changed is its urgency: it is no longer on the MVP's critical path, because the MVP's adapter does not need it.

OQ-23 · Which provider CLIs, and what must each guarantee?

Why it is open: D-033 says "the provider's command-line tool" without naming one or saying what it must be capable of. Every tool in this category has different flags, different output, different session semantics, and no stability guarantee for programmatic use — R-12.

The answer needs to be an adapter contract before it is a list of tools. At minimum each supported CLI must: run fully non-interactively from a working directory with no prompt for input; accept a file-based instruction rather than only an argument string; exit with a code that distinguishes a refusal from a crash from a completed run; leave its output on disk rather than only on a terminal; and be pinnable to a version with a smoke test that fails loudly when the tool changes underneath it. Anything that cannot do those five is not a supported adapter, however capable it is interactively.

The second half is which tools clear that bar on day one, which cannot be answered from this repository — it needs each one tried. That is a small, cheap experiment and it should precede the first adapter being written, not follow it.

Narrowed by D-036. A tool that fails the contract is no longer a dead end for the operator who needs that provider: API mode is a supported configuration rather than a hypothetical exit, so the answer to "this CLI cannot be driven safely" is a setting rather than a fork. What remains genuinely open is the list, and the version pinning and boot smoke test that keep a passing tool from silently stopping being one.

Narrowed further by D-041. With multi-provider decided and CLI mode the MVP's focus, the five-point contract above stops being a description of one tool and becomes the gate that decides which providers ship first — a vendor is in the MVP if its command-line tool clears it and reachable through API mode if it does not. That raises the priority of the cheap experiment named above rather than changing its shape: the list is now on the critical path, not beside it.

Answered as D-049 and D-050: the cheap experiment was run on 2026-08-21 against @anthropic-ai/claude-code@2.1.238, and it is the first supported adapter. It clears four of the five points cleanly. It does not clear the third — and that is the finding worth having had before an adapter was written rather than after. Crashes and completed runs are distinguishable; refusals are not. A task the model could not do exits 0 exactly as one it did, so a fifth point had to become policy rather than a property of the tool: the adapter reads the outcome from the mandatory handoff document and never from the exit code (D-050). The contract itself survives the contact unchanged, which is the useful result — it was written strictly enough that a real tool failed it in a specific, fixable place instead of passing vacuously.

What it opened: nothing new on this page, but two standing obligations. The version pin is inert without the boot smoke test, which lands with T-057; and the list is still one entry long, so R-08 does not fall and R-12 is untouched — neither is a question this repository can close by measurement.

OQ-13 · Who runs the acceptance tests, and how often?

Why it was open: the design specifies three tests that are the only honest proof the system works — the unsatisfiable task, the fold-from-genesis comparison, and the second run a week apart. It did not say who runs them, on what cadence, or what happens when one fails. The fold test in particular is described as periodic, which implies automation nobody had scheduled.

Answered as D-025: the product owner runs everything, since no CI exists and every internal stakeholder role is currently one person. Full test scenarios, positive and negative, for the deploy automations that run today and the three acceptance tests that do not yet: QA test plan.

OQ-16 · Is high availability required, and for whom?

Why it is open: HA is discussed in the data model and deferred, with the event stream named as the second recovery path — a restore missing the last few minutes can be rolled forward from events. That is a real mitigation and not the same as availability.

Narrowed by D-031, which removed most of it. GAOS hosts nothing and promises no uptime, so availability is each operator's own capacity decision, and R-09 stops being commercially blocking. What remains is genuinely smaller and still needs an answer: whether the project's own two tiers need better than one node — they carry the docs, the demo and the owner's own runs — and whether HA guidance ships as documentation, as candidate (C) in OQ-19, or not at all.

OQ-18 · Budget caps: initial values and behaviour at the limit

Why it was open: budget_cap is specified per workflow and per tenant and implemented nowhere. Two things needed deciding — the initial values, and the behaviour when one is reached. Stopping mid-workflow leaves a half-finished task graph; refusing to dispatch the next task is cleaner but slower to notice; warning and continuing is not a cap.

Answered as D-037, and the answer is that one number with one behaviour was the wrong shape. The cap becomes a three-zone guard with pacing and deferral, specified in full at BRD §8.10.1:

  • Pacing — the ceiling in force at any moment tracks how much of the budget period has elapsed, times a slack multiplier. Without it a monthly cap can be spent in an afternoon, which is the same as having no cap for the other twenty-nine days.
  • Deferral, not failure — at the throttle threshold, default 80%, dispatch stops and ready work is deferred with a resume time. The task keeps its place in the graph, releases its lease, and comes back on its own when the window advances. Failing it instead would waste the money already spent and then invite a retry that spends more of the budget that just ran out.
  • A hard stop that refuses rather than kills — at 100% nothing new is dispatched and a deliberate act is needed to continue, but runs already in flight are always left alone, because killing them forfeits spend and leaves a half-written workspace.

The values turned out to be the easy half: the DDL already defaults to 500 USD and 8 concurrent tasks, and the five new properties ship with defaults that make a fresh install safe rather than permissive. They are deployment-tunable configuration in software the operator runs, not a published commitment, so committing to them costs nothing — unlike the rate card in OQ-21, which is why that one is still open and this one is not.

Two things this answer does not settle. It depends on OQ-22 for how far an estimate may drift, since estimates count toward the guard at full weight — a cap that ignored them would be no cap at all in CLI mode. And it adds four event names and a task state to a closed catalog, which under BR-05 is a schema change to the fold and lands in three places together or not at all.

Nothing here is rhetorical

Every question on this page exists because answering it changes what gets built. None of them is a placeholder, and none can be resolved by reading the repository more carefully — that has already been done, and the absence is the finding. The seventeen that are answered stayed on the page for the same reason: the options that were rejected are the part that gets forgotten first.
